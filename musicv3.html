Section III-A — Reading Comprehension

The Musical Past and the Nineteenth Century

During much of the eighteenth century, musical institutions regularly required new compositions. Courts, churches, opera houses, and concert organizations expected composers to produce music for immediate use. A successful work did not necessarily remain in the repertory for generations.

During the nineteenth century, however, older works became increasingly important in concert life. Conservatories trained professional performers, concert organizations expanded, and critics began to describe certain works as permanent artistic monuments.

This created a new challenge for composers. A symphonist working after Beethoven did not simply have to compose a successful new piece. The composer also worked in the shadow of an established repertory that audiences already considered authoritative.

Some musicians therefore looked to the past for permanent artistic values, while others believed that the history of music demonstrated a need for continued progress.

1. According to the passage, eighteenth-century institutions often required:
Correct answer: a. new compositions
2. What became increasingly important during the nineteenth century?
Older music / an established historical repertory became increasingly important.
3. In the passage, which composer represents the weight of the established symphonic tradition?
Beethoven.
4. In one or two sentences, explain the tension described in the final paragraph.
A strong answer should explain the tension between treating the past as a source of permanent artistic values and viewing history as evidence that music should continue to progress.
